When Beliefs Move Markets: A Behavioural Explanation of Financial Bubbles

Kassym Medina-Zeynep

A bubble arises from a contradictory phenomenon: assets are worth more because their price is expected to increase, and they do, in fact, rise in value for as long as enough people believe the price will increase. That investors’ growing optimism attains a degree of objectivity and is finally externalized as a force of the market is the core of the above-mentioned phenomenon.

That the class of financial bubbles extending from the Dutch Tulipmania of 1637 to the more recent phenomenon of the bubble in Bitcoin prices experienced by investors in 2017, should persist as a class of market failures through so many epochs, is a puzzle not easily solved by reference to the considerable literature on the subject. Finance theory long ago assumed that markets are efficient in the sense that they Correctly Price all relevant information. And many economists since have either deemed it impossible for a bubble to occur or, when it has occurred, explained it away by reference to the rational behavior of “buy and hold” investors who expected others to drive up prices before they sold out (Fama, 1970; Blanchard and Jacobs, 2009). That such bubbles occur so frequently suggests that there must be more to the persistence of bubbles than is contained in these understandings of how markets work.

First, as investors enter a financial bubble in anticipation of rising prices, their buying helps drive up the price. As prices rise, further evidence surfaces that appears to confirm their initial perceptions about expectations. Many investors engage in herding behavior whereby they follow the actions of other investors. In turn, as prices continue to rise, more investors enter the market in anticipation of future price increases by other investors (Shiller, 2000).

In addition to these two patterns, another important aspect that extends a bubble to its bursting point are overconfidence and loss aversion. Overconfidence drives active traders to a higher extent of trading, and thereby they underestimate the risks associated with their transactions. As shown in research by Barber and Odean (2000), e.g. at a discount-brokerage, the very active traders even lose money. Their average annual returns reached -11.4%, whereas the market returned 17.9% over the same time frame. As discussed in the work of prospect theory by Kahneman and Tversky (1979), loss aversion on the other hand plays a big role in the reverse direction of a bubble. Once the asset price has started to drop, investors become overly sensitive to potential losses, and therefore tend to sell off their holdings in a frantic attempt to avoid further loses.

Relatedly, the stock of dollars and the scale of leverage have structural features which support particular ‘mental maps’ of financial markets. Thus for instance the low interest-rate environment combined with cheap credit has become a medium for the mass transmission of behavioral tendencies to the population as a whole and to become systemic (Mian & Sufi, 2009).

One of the things that is striking about financial bubbles is that humans all work off the same basic set of cognitions to evaluate investment vehicles. Tulip manias, dot-com booms, housing price bubbles, and Bitcoin manias are all easily explained by one human psychological feedback loop regardless of the economic fundamentals of the assets in question. So, why do financial bubbles keep happening? The reason is that the same human psychology that explains bubble behavior in the 17th century continues to operate today. As markets and new investment vehicles are added to the landscape of financial behavior, the core mechanism of beliefs confirming beliefs and triggering reversals remains stable and persistent.
